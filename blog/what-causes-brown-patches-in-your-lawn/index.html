What Causes Brown Spots and Patches in Your Lawn?

Updated November 1, 2026  ·  Lawn Disease  ·  By Mike Harmon, Owner & Licensed Applicator

Brown spots in an Indiana lawn are usually caused by one of these: lawn disease (most often brown patch fungus in summer), grubs eating the roots, drought, dog urine, mowing with a dull blade or too short, a fertilizer spill, or poor soil left from construction. In late winter, snow mold is common too. Each cause needs a different fix, so figure out which one you have before you water, fertilize, or spray anything.

It isn't always disease. Purdue's turf disease specialist says that in half or more of the lawn samples sent to their lab, a fungus isn't the cause at all. Here's how to tell the causes apart.

Purdue offers a simple first check. Damage from things like drought, spills, or bad soil doesn't spread from plant to plant. It shows up quickly and in a more even pattern. Disease spreads and looks patchier.

Brown Patch Disease

What it looks like: Straw-colored or brown circular patches, from a few inches to several feet across. Look closely at the blades for irregular tan spots with a dark brown border.

When it shows up: During hot, humid stretches in summer. Purdue's lawn disease guide (BP-218) says it takes off when nights stay at 68°F or higher and days reach 86°F or higher. Purdue's brown patch profile (BP-106) adds that it needs long periods of dew on the grass.

Who gets it: Tall fescue lawns. Purdue calls brown patch the disease that most limits tall fescue in Indiana. Kentucky bluegrass is much less likely to get it.

The fix: Water early in the morning, never in the evening. Avoid fertilizing in summer. Raise the mowing height on tall fescue to 3.5 to 4 inches during hot weather. Brown patch doesn't kill the crowns or roots, so the grass usually grows back once the weather cools. Purdue notes that most fungicides sold to homeowners aren't very effective, so severe or repeat cases are worth a professional treatment.

Other Lawn Diseases That Cause Brown Spots

  • Dollar spot: Small, round, straw-colored spots that can grow to 6 inches or more and run together. Purdue says it's worse on underfertilized lawns, and a light nitrogen feeding usually helps. Tall fescue doesn't get it.
  • Summer patch: Brown rings or "frog-eye" patches from 6 inches to 3 feet across, mostly on Kentucky bluegrass in summer. The grass pulls up easily, and the roots are black and rotted. Purdue recommends keeping the mowing height at 3 inches or more.
  • Red thread: Tan or pink patches 4 to 8 inches across, with thin red or pink threads on the blades. Purdue says it's mostly cosmetic, and proper fertilization usually clears it up.

Grub Damage

What it looks like: Irregular patches of wilted or brown grass that don't green up when you water. Purdue's grub guide (E-271) says the damage can show up any time from August through November. Skunks, raccoons, or birds digging at night are another sign.

How to check: Grab the brown grass and pull. If it peels up like loose carpet, grubs have eaten the roots. To count them, cut out a square foot of sod about 3 inches deep and look for white, C-shaped larvae. Purdue says fewer than 5 grubs per square foot rarely needs treatment. Around 5 to 10 per square foot is usually where damage starts.

The fix: A curative grub treatment, then reseed the dead areas. A preventive treatment in early summer stops the next round before it starts. See our full guide on how to tell if your lawn has grubs.

Drought and Summer Dormancy

What it looks like: Large areas, or the whole lawn, turning tan or blue-gray without clear patch shapes. Footprints stay visible after you walk across it.

Is it dead? Usually not. Brown, dormant grass is how cool-season grass survives a dry spell. Purdue says dormant turf can survive 5 to 8 weeks without water and typically recovers within 1 to 2 weeks after real rain returns.

The fix: Water deeply or let it stay dormant, but don't switch back and forth. Stay off it as much as you can, including mowing. Don't spray weed killer on a drought-stressed lawn, because Purdue says it doesn't work well on stressed weeds and can damage stressed grass. Our watering guide covers how much and when.

Dog Urine Spots

What it looks like: Purdue's guide to dog urine damage (AY-327) describes a brown center 3 to 6 inches across, surrounded by a dark green ring 6 to 12 inches across. The salts and nitrogen in the urine burn the center. The diluted edge acts like fertilizer, which is why the ring is greener than the rest of the lawn.

The fix: Pour water on the spot soon after the dog goes. For dead spots, rake out the dead grass, loosen the soil, and reseed. Purdue says the dog food supplements sold to prevent urine spots haven't been proven to work.

Dull Mower Blades and Mowing Too Short

Purdue's mowing guide (AY-8) says improper mowing causes more lawn problems than any other maintenance practice. A dull blade tears the grass instead of cutting it, which leaves a whitish or brown cast across the whole lawn. Mowing too short weakens the roots and makes the lawn more likely to suffer from disease, insects, and drought.

The fix: Sharpen the blade at least twice a season. Never cut off more than a third of the grass blade at once. Purdue's recommended heights for Indiana lawn grasses top out at 3.5 to 4 inches, and we mow at 3.75 to 4 inches all season. More on that in are you cutting your lawn too short?

Fertilizer and Chemical Spills

What it looks like: Brown stripes that follow your spreader path, overlapping arcs, or a single dead spot where product spilled or the spreader sat.

Why it happens: A Purdue plant diagnostic article explains that the high salt content in fertilizer draws water out of the grass, which burns and kills it.

The fix: Pick up as much spilled product as you can (a shop vacuum works), then flush the area with plenty of water. If the grass is already dead, Purdue says the only fix is usually to reseed or lay sod.

Poor Soil and Buried Construction Debris

What it looks like: The same spots turn brown every summer, often along sidewalks, driveways, or curbs. Purdue says these spots often have gravel or sand left over from construction under them. In newer neighborhoods, builders often strip the topsoil and spread only an inch or two back before seeding.

How to check: Purdue's test: if you can't push a screwdriver into the soil, roots can't get through it either. Dig a few inches down and look for rock, sand, or clay.

The fix: Purdue recommends core aeration and adding compost to improve the soil. For spots with buried rock or debris, dig out the debris, add topsoil, and reseed. Our post on repairing a lawn after construction covers the steps.

Snow Mold in Late Winter

What it looks like: Matted, gray or tan circles 6 to 12 inches across that show up as the snow melts. Pink snow mold has a pink or salmon-colored edge. Gray snow mold needs snow cover, so it's worst under snow piles along driveways and sidewalks.

The fix: Purdue says snow mold is largely cosmetic on home lawns and fungicides aren't recommended. Rake the matted grass in spring to help it dry out, and it will usually recover on its own. To prevent it, keep mowing in fall until the grass stops growing, don't put down heavy nitrogen within six weeks of the first hard freeze, and avoid piling snow on the same patch of lawn all winter.

Brown Spots in Lawn FAQ

What causes brown spots in a lawn?
The most common causes in Indiana are lawn disease (especially brown patch in summer), grubs, drought, dog urine, mowing with a dull blade or too short, fertilizer spills, and poor soil from construction. Snow mold causes brown or gray spots in late winter.

Should I water brown spots in my lawn?
Only if the cause is drought. If footprints stay visible and the browning is spread across the lawn, water deeply. If the spots are circular with dark edges, extra water, especially in the evening, can make brown patch disease worse.

Will brown spots in grass grow back?
Often, yes. Grass that's brown from drought or brown patch disease usually recovers, because the crowns and roots survive. Grass killed by grubs, dog urine, or a fertilizer spill usually needs to be reseeded.

How do I know if brown spots are from grubs?
Pull on the brown grass. If it lifts up like loose carpet with no roots, grubs are likely. Cut back a square foot of sod about 3 inches deep and count the white, C-shaped grubs. Purdue says fewer than 5 per square foot rarely needs treatment.

Why does my lawn get brown spots every summer?
Spots that come back every year usually point to something that keeps happening: evening watering, summer fertilizing, mowing too short, a tall fescue lawn that's prone to brown patch, or poor soil and buried debris in the same spots. Fixing that underlying condition matters more than treating the spots each year.

What causes brown patches in a lawn in winter or early spring?
Usually snow mold, which shows up as matted gray or pinkish circles as the snow melts. It's mostly cosmetic. Rake the matted grass in spring and it usually recovers on its own.
